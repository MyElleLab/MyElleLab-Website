Somewhere around 1954, a wealthy Canadian couple started playing a dice game
aboard their yacht. They called it the Yacht Game. Friends who played it wanted
copies of their own, so the couple approached a New York toy manufacturer named
Edwin S. Lowe and asked him to produce a few sets they could give away.

Lowe had form. He had spotted a rudimentary game called Beano at a Georgia
carnival in 1929, renamed it Bingo, and built a company on it. He looked at the
Yacht Game and offered to buy the rights outright.

The couple agreed. Their only condition was that they receive the first
thousand sets, so they could keep giving the game to friends.

Lowe registered the name Yahtzee as a trademark in 1956. The game went on to
sell tens of millions of copies. Milton Bradley bought his company in 1973, and
Hasbro owns it today.

The couple were never named. No one has ever come forward to claim it.

## What they were actually building on

The yacht story is good, and it is also incomplete. Yahtzee did not appear from
nowhere.

A game called Yacht was published in 1938 with the same essential shape: five
dice, a scorecard split between plain number counts and poker-style
combinations. South America had Generala. Scandinavia had Yatzy. A game called
Yatzie appeared in a 1943 compendium set.

Yahtzee is the most famous branch of that tree, not its root. What the couple
did, and what Lowe recognised, was arrive at a version that balanced well
enough to be played for seventy years.

## The rules, in one pass

Five dice. Thirteen turns. On each turn you roll all five, then may reroll any
of them up to twice more, keeping whichever you like between rolls. At the end
of the turn you must write a score into one of thirteen categories, and once a
category is used it is closed for the rest of the game.

The scorecard has two halves.

**Upper section.** Six boxes, one for each number. You score the sum of the
dice showing that number. Three fours in the fours box scores twelve. If your
upper section totals 63 or more, you get a 35 point bonus, which is exactly the
total you reach by scoring three of every number.

**Lower section.** Seven boxes with poker logic:

- Three of a kind: the sum of all five dice
- Four of a kind: the sum of all five dice
- Full house: 25 points
- Small straight, four consecutive numbers: 30 points
- Large straight, five consecutive numbers: 40 points
- Yahtzee, five of a kind: 50 points
- Chance: the sum of all five dice, whatever they are

A second Yahtzee in the same game is worth a 100 point bonus.

## Why it survived

The rules fit on the back of the box, and the interesting decisions do not.

The real game is not in the rolling. It is in what you do with a turn that went
badly. You have three fives and nothing else, and you have already used your
fives. Do you take fifteen in three of a kind and lose the category, or do you
write a zero somewhere cheap and keep your options open?

Every turn forces a commitment, and every commitment closes a door. By turn
nine you are not trying to roll well, you are managing the damage from turns
one through eight. That structure is why a game with almost no rules holds up
against games with a hundred.

The chance box is the clearest piece of design in it. It exists purely to
absorb a bad turn, and it is usually the thing you regret spending first.

## The part the couple got right

They could have patented it, named it after themselves, and taken a royalty.
Instead they traded the whole thing for a thousand copies to give to friends,
which tells you roughly what they were optimising for.

Seventy years later the game is still being played, mostly by people who have
never heard of Edwin Lowe and would not recognise a yacht if they saw one.
