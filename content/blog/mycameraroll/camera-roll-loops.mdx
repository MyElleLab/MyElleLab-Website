How many times today have you taken your phone out of your pocket? You do not
need to count. It is a lot, and some of those times you filmed something: the
coffee, the dog doing the thing it does, a sky that only lasted a minute.

A few years ago that would have meant carrying a camera. Now the best camera is
the one you already have, and it is good enough that you no longer think about
whether a moment is worth filming. You just film it.

That is the good news, and it is also how the problem starts.

## Why the phone won

A dedicated camera can still beat a phone on paper. It rarely matters, because
the dedicated camera is in a drawer when the double rainbow appears. The phone
is in your hand.

It also does a great deal of the work for you. Modern phones combine several
exposures into one picture, steady the shot while you walk, and film clean 4K
video in light that would have been hopeless a decade ago. You do not need to
know any of that to get a clip that looks good, which is exactly the point.

So people film more: time lapses of clouds, slow motion of a workout, a recipe
step, a friend's first try at something. The camera stopped being the limit a
long time ago.

## The roll that swallows everything

The limit now is the camera roll itself. It is a single list, sorted by date,
where a clip from a holiday sits between a screenshot of a train time and a
meme someone sent you. A clip is easy to find in the week you filmed it and
almost impossible six months later, unless you remember roughly when it
happened and are willing to scroll there.

Most people are not. The clips get watched once, on the day, and then they sink
under everything that arrives after them. The roll stops being a library and
becomes storage.

## Collections first, then loops

What helps is less clever than it sounds. Put the clips you care about into a
handful of collections that mean something to you: a trip, a person, the
exercises you are learning, the recipes you keep coming back to. Five clips you
return to are worth more than five hundred you scroll past.

Then notice which clips you watch more than once. Some are made for repeating:
the form on a lift you are practising, the one tricky step in a recipe, a
thirty second moment you just like. Those are the ones that benefit from a loop,
a single clip on repeat or a whole collection played through as a playlist.

## What to keep watching

None of this needs a new habit of filming less. Keep filming whatever catches
your eye. The change is in what happens afterwards: a minute spent moving the
good clips somewhere you will look again. The camera already caught the moment.
The collection is what lets you see it twice.
