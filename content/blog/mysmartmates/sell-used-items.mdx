Look around the room you are in. Somewhere there is a drawer of cables for
devices you no longer own, a jacket you have not worn in two winters, a
speaker, a lens, a lamp. Most homes have a shelf of things that still work and
that someone else would pay for.

How much it adds up to depends on who is counting. A
[2019 survey for Mercari](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/study-reveals-clutter-epidemic-cause-low-sell-esteem-300814777.html)
of 1,000 American adults put it at about 42 unused items worth around $723 per
household. Estimates from the resale platforms themselves run much higher. The
market that would take those items is large either way:
[ThredUp's 2025 resale report](https://newsroom.thredup.com/news/thredup-13th-resale-report)
expects secondhand clothing alone to reach $367 billion worldwide by 2029.

Plenty of people do sell. In the
[OfferUp Recommerce Report 2025](https://www.recommercereport.com/), 54 per cent
of American adults said they had sold a pre-owned item in the past year. The
rest have usually tried, or thought about trying, and stopped at the same
place.

## Where the time goes

In the Mercari survey, 65 per cent of people said selling was too hard, took
too long or was a hassle, and the most common reason was not knowing what their
things were worth. That matches what anyone who has tried will tell you. The
listing itself is quick. The research before it is not.

| Step | What makes it slow |
| --- | --- |
| Identifying the item | The tag says the brand, not the model, the year or the variant that sets the price |
| Pricing it | Comparable listings vary with condition, and a price that is too high sits while one that is too low leaves money behind |
| Choosing where | Clothes sell on one platform, electronics on another, furniture locally, and it changes by country |

Each step on its own is manageable. Together, repeated for every item in a
box, they are why the box goes back into the cupboard.

## The Saturday that started it

MySellingMate did not start in a meeting. It started during a weekend cleanout,
when a few of us spent a Saturday surrounded by boxes of audio gear, jackets
and tech we no longer used.

We opened tab after tab, compared sold listings, and argued over what a fair
price was. By five in the afternoon we had spent four hours and listed two
items.

![An illustration of a person sitting on a living room floor, sorting clothes, books and gadgets into clear storage boxes](/blog/sell-used-items-sorting.jpg)

There had to be a better way to turn a physical item into a price and a place
to list it. That frustration became MySellingMate.

## One photo instead of a stack of tabs

The idea is to turn the three slow steps into one. You photograph the item. The
app works out what it is, estimates a fair price from comparable used listings
in your local currency, and suggests where it is likely to sell in your region.

It also had to respect what people keep in their homes. There is no account to
create and no ad tracking. Your scan history and estimated values stay on your
iPhone. The photo itself is sent for analysis through a server in the EU, as
the app's privacy policy sets out.

## The box in the cupboard

The things in that box are not going to become more valuable by waiting, and
most of them will quietly become less. If the only thing standing between them
and someone who wants them is an afternoon of research, that afternoon is worth
shortening.
