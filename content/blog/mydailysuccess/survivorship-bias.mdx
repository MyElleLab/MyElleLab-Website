Find what is uncommon. That has been my mantra for a long time, and it is
probably why survivorship bias caught my attention the first time I came across
it.

I was reading a post on an old social media where users could ask questions and
comment the answers by people, and a picture of an airplane stopped me. It was
the cover image of a post. The plane was covered in dots, scattered across the
wings and the fuselage, and clustered nowhere in particular. I did not
understand what I was looking at, so I read the post.

The dots were bullet holes.

During the second world war, a group of mathematicians at Columbia University
were asked to help the American military reduce bomber losses. The obvious
approach was to look at the planes coming back, map where they had been hit,
and add armour there. That is what the data showed. The wings and the fuselage
took the damage.

Abraham Wald, a Hungarian mathematician working with that group, said the
opposite. The armour belonged where the returning planes had no holes at all.
Around the engines. Around the cockpit.

The reason is simple once you see it, and almost impossible to see before.
Every plane they were measuring had come back. The holes on those planes marked
the places a bomber could be hit and still fly home. The planes hit in the
engine were not in the sample, because they were at the bottom of the channel.

The data was not wrong. The data was complete, accurate, and describing a
population that had already been filtered.

## Where it took me

That post sent me to Daniel Kahneman's Thinking, Fast and Slow, which I read
slowly and found uncomfortable in the way good books are. What stayed with me
was not any single bias. It was the realisation that most of them are not
failures of intelligence. They are failures of sampling. You reason correctly
about the wrong set.

Survivorship bias is the cleanest example. Nobody in that room was being
careless. They had real data, carefully collected, and they were about to
armour exactly the wrong parts of the plane.

## Why I care about data

I work with data for a living. In my research it is energy systems and
environmental impacts, and in my apps it is much smaller things. Either way the
same thing is true: the model is only as good as the sample, and the sample is
almost never what you assume.

But there is a second reason, and it is more personal.

Statistics, machine learning, and now AI are all in the business of finding
patterns in numbers. That is the professional version. The everyday version is
much smaller and, for me, more useful. When you keep a record of something you
do, you eventually see a pattern in it. Not a dramatic one. A slow one.

That slow pattern is what keeps me going. Motivation is nothing if you can't
see the results of your effort. What I trust is being able to look back at a
year and see that a line moved. Not because anyone told me it would, but
because I can see it.

## The record I keep

This is part of why we built MyGrowth. I wanted to log habits and then look at
the statistics, and I wanted to be able to go back years rather than weeks. I
still open it to compare a month against the same month two years ago, which is
a strange thing to enjoy, and I do enjoy it.

The connection to the bombers is direct. Every article about someone else's
routine is a returning plane. It is a real record of something that worked, and
it tells you nothing about the ones that did not come back. Your own log is the
only record you will ever have that contains your failures alongside your
successes.

It is a small sample. But it is not a filtered one, and that turns out to
matter more.
