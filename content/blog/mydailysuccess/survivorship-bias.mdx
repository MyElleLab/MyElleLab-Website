Every morning routine you have read about comes from someone it worked for.
That is the whole problem.

The person who woke at five, wrote for an hour, and built a career on it has a
story worth telling and a reason to tell it. The person who tried the same
schedule for three weeks, slept badly, and went back to waking at seven wrote
nothing down. There was nothing to write. Failure at a habit is not a story,
it is an absence, and absences do not get published.

So the advice arrives pre-filtered. Not falsified, not exaggerated, just
incomplete in one specific direction: you see the cases where the method and
the person happened to fit, and none of the cases where they did not.

## The filter runs more than once

Survivorship bias is usually drawn as a single cut: the sample you can see,
and the sample removed before you saw it. In self-improvement the cut is
unusually deep, because it happens several times over.

- Habits that fail early leave no artefact. Nobody writes up the cold plunge
  they abandoned on day four.
- Habits that fail slowly get reframed. A routine that quietly stopped working
  is remembered as a phase rather than a result.
- The people best placed to write about a method are the ones it suited, which
  is the least representative sample available.
- Distribution finishes the job. A routine that produced an ordinary outcome
  does not travel, so the surviving accounts skew toward the extremes.

Each pass removes the same kind of evidence: the ordinary, the partial, the
abandoned. What is left is a library of methods that all appear to work,
written by the people for whom they did.

## What it costs you

The damage is not that you try something useless. Most published routines are
reasonable, and some of them will suit you.

The damage is to your sense of the base rate. When every account you read ends
in success, the implied success rate sits close to one, and a method that
fails you starts to look like a personal failing rather than an ordinary
outcome. That reading is wrong, and it is expensive. It pushes people to
repeat a method harder instead of dropping it, and to treat a bad fit as a
defect in themselves.

> Advice can tell you a method worked for someone. It cannot tell you how many
> people it did not work for, because those people are not in the sample.

The correction is not general scepticism. It is remembering what the evidence
actually supports: that the method is possible, not that it is likely.

## The one record that is not filtered

Your own history is the only unfiltered evidence you have about what works for
you. It includes the attempts that failed, which no external source will ever
hand you, and it is drawn from a sample of exactly the person you are trying
to advise.

That is a much smaller dataset than the internet's, and a much better one for
this particular question. It has three properties the published version lacks:

- It contains the failures. You know which routines you abandoned, and roughly
  when you stopped.
- It is specific to your constraints: your sleep, your job, your commute, the
  people you live with.
- It was not written to persuade anyone, so nothing has been rounded off.

Keeping it is unglamorous. A date, what you did, whether it held, and the
point at which it stopped is enough. The value is not in any single entry. It
is in being able to look back across six months and see a pattern that no
account of someone else's morning could have given you.

None of this makes the published routines worthless. Read them as a catalogue
of what has been tried, not as a ranking of what works, and check the result
against the only record that includes your failures.
