When people decide to take better care of themselves, they usually plan
something large. A new morning routine that starts at five, an hour of
meditation, a diet, a gym plan, all beginning on Monday.

By the third week most of it has gone, and the conclusion people draw is that
they lack discipline. The more likely explanation is that the plan was built for
a different person with a different week.

What lasts tends to be much smaller, and repeated.

## Ten minutes before the phone

Many mornings start the same way: the alarm, the phone in your hand, and the
day's messages arriving before you are fully awake. You spend the first part of
the morning reacting to other people's priorities.

Your body is already busy in that window. Cortisol rises on its own in the half
hour after waking, the
[cortisol awakening response](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cortisol_awakening_response),
as part of getting you up and running. Filling the same half hour with
notifications is a choice, and it is an easy one to change.

Try giving the first ten minutes to something slower. Drink a glass of water.
Open a window or step outside for daylight. Decide on one thing the day is for.
It is a small anchor, and a small anchor is one you will still be using next
month.

## Pauses that fit into the day

Mindfulness has a reputation for needing a cushion and an hour. It works in
much smaller pieces.

Take two minutes with your first coffee or tea instead of drinking it over your
inbox. Set a gentle reminder every hour and a half or so to stand up, stretch
and look at something further away than a screen. None of this will change
your life on a Tuesday. It changes what the average Tuesday feels like, which
is the thing you actually live in.

## Plan for the days you have

Energy is not the same every day. It moves with sleep, stress, the season and a
dozen things you do not control. A routine that assumes you will feel the same
on every day of the year breaks on the first bad one, and the guilt that
follows does more damage than the missed session.

Plan in two gears instead. On days with energy, do the demanding work, the hard
workout, the creative project. On days without it, keep the habit alive in a
lighter form: a walk instead of a run, tidying instead of planning, rest that
you count as rest rather than failure.

## Write it down, because you will not feel it

The hardest part of small habits is that their progress is invisible from one
day to the next. A glass of water, a short walk, five minutes of reading: on any
single day none of them looks like it matters, and that is exactly when people
stop.

A written record fixes the part your memory cannot. A
[2016 meta-analysis of 138 studies](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/87431/)
found that monitoring progress reliably helps people reach their goals, and
that the effect is stronger when the progress is physically recorded. Seeing
that you kept a habit on twenty days out of the last thirty is a better reason
to continue than any amount of motivation.

## The size of it

None of this looks like transformation, and that is the point. Big resets ask
you to become someone else by Monday. Small choices ask you to be slightly kinder
to the person you already are, today, and then again tomorrow.
